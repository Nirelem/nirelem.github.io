**Trigger Warnings:** Mild gore (at beginning), character death

&emsp;She is found a disfiguration of herself. She is found sprawled over dirt and concrete, her arms
and legs—branches of meat and claw and mold—cracked, her hair—mycelium networks and slicked
fur and tangled vines—splayed open. Her belly unfurls, flowering in a reach towards the moon. Her
intestines are a cluster of knotted garbage bags and plastic straws, her ribs a flash of carbon-steel teeth,
filled with wet, bubbling, festering cement.

&emsp;Her face reminds me she is still herself. I look at her face and she is still the angel. Still
sienna-skinned and onyx-eyed, still divine, even as her skin molts to gray and her eyes melt to tar. Still
the angel, the fairy, the lady of the field.

&emsp;Still beautiful.

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;“Why aren’t you asleep?” Wiping my eyes, I try to suppress a yawn.

&emsp;“I—*what*? After that? You saw her too!”

&emsp; I pull the blanket over my eyes. Goodnight.

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;My mother’s memory of her is sparse. My father’s, even sparser. Stories of her trickle to fine
sand, slipping between the fingers of time and out the tongues of neighbours and shopkeepers and
grandparents, who heard stories from their grandparents, their memories weathered with time. Still,
each story is a story nonetheless, and, as Leo would say, each story comes from a memory, and each
memory must come from *somewhere*. Truth, usually.

&emsp;So: stories were memories. And though each memory was sparse and scattered, specks of
distant stars, there was still *a* connection. He always had a way of stringing together constellations.

&emsp;The story goes like this: There is a lady, or perhaps a fairy, or even an angel, and she lives in the
field across town. There is a lady, and every night, or perhaps every day, or even every hour, she kneels
amongst the grass and the wildflowers and the shrubbery, closes her eyes, and tips her face to the sun.
There is a lady, and sometimes, she weaves clouds to coax rain and threads together root and twig and
bark to encourage trees.

&emsp;The truth goes like this: There has *always* been a lady, and there has *always* been a field, and
there has *never* been one without the other.

&emsp;Outside, the field, once-open and since-sectioned—closed off from the rest of the world with
tarpaulin advertisements and rust-rotten wire fences and paint-peeling signs in a language I could not
understand—bleeds dirt and drools concrete.

&emsp;The story remains the same.

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;“Come with me!”

&emsp;“Sneak *out* with you, you mean.”

&emsp;“So *boring*. She’s nice once you get to know her, promise!”

&emsp;“Tomorrow, promise.”

&emsp;Beneath me, my fingers curl over intermediate paper and black pen. In front of me, the light of
the screen burns onto my face, and I pray so does the information it displays. Above: the whirr of a fan,
the rustle of a rodent. Behind:

&emsp;“You always say that,”

&emsp;“Don’t you have homework to do?”

&emsp;A stuck out tongue.

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;Outside, a typhoon tries to tear the world apart. It is hungry, hungrier than my memory has
ever supported, and angrier than my parents said it could be. Rain begs at my window, a barrage of
bullets against glass. Lightning lashes in the distance, the prelude to thunder’s roar. Overhead, the
lights begin to hum—cicadas in summer—then flicker, then—

&emsp;Damn it. I had to finish that video *tonight*.

&emsp;Instinct pulls me to my phone. Blue light greets me once more as I flick through news outlets
and government announcements and group chat notifications. I pray for my car’s health tomorrow.
After a moment: my own. I will not let sickness take me too.

&emsp;Eventually, I shut off my phone and lean against my chair. Distantly—downstairs, I think—I
push away the sounds of my parents’ phones—the drone of news, of transmission lost in
translation—and allow myself to be pulled to my window. The curtains peel open, pressed aside by my
fingers, and

&emsp;She is impossible to miss.

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;Memory is rooted in truth. So is a story.

&emsp;Once upon a time, Leo and I went on an adventure. Well—he called it an adventure. I called it
a budding arrest. Regardless, once upon a time, as the night continued to breathe and bleed into a
deeper and deeper darkness, we slipped out of our house, scaled the wall of our village (the cracks were
easy enough ledges), and snuck into the field. Once upon a time, the field was still a field, and not a
complex in creation.

&emsp;I remember crawling under the wires, holding my breath so as not to inhale any of the loose
dirt from the hole Leo dug—*apparently*—a year ago. He said it made him feel like a dog, scratching and
tugging and pulling at the earth to discover hidden treasure. Wasn’t that so *cool*? I looked at him,
grinning tooth-gapped and bright-eyed, imagined a tiny, furry tail wagging wildly behind him, and
forgot I wanted to scold him—for sneaking out for *months*, for doing *this* instead of his homework, for
risking his health when he *knew*—

&emsp;Instead, I laughed, and watched him *beam*.

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;Memory is rooted in mourning.

&emsp;On the morning of Leo’s wake, I bid my parents goodbye, tolerated the embraces of forgotten
family, and slipped out, away, and into my car. I do not remember the drive, but I remember my baby
brother’s voice, catching it in the laughter of radio broadcasters, plucking it from each *oh really*, and
*why not*. In advertisements for countries I’ll never get a chance to visit—*don’t you want to escape? tired*
*of the rain? the heat? the world? why not run*—I hear nothing but his imagined itineraries and dozens of
now-discarded dreams.

&emsp;My hands begin to shake. My throat grows dry. My heart, beating as desperate as the rain.
Outside, I watch window-wipers sweep away rain-stained streaks. Already, my hand is raised to echo it,
and as my fist presses against trickling tears—budding beneath my eyes and bleeding down my face—all
it does is smear over my cheek.

&emsp;I cannot afford this.

&emsp;On the morning of my brother’s wake, I replace memory with memorisation, and drive to take
my midterm.

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;My memory of her is sparse. I could never be my brother, never share his mind or his youth or
his passion, or the love he laced in every breath he took, but I could try to be. A poor imitation, at least.
Though, memory was rooted in truth, and here was the truth:

&emsp;If not for her face, I would not have recognised her. One look at her torn flesh and unravelling
skin, at the growing pool of murky, muddy water, stretching so wide and so far as to slip through my
shoes and seep into my socks, and I would have left. Even the *smell*—mold and mildew; a persistent,
pestilent, *rot*—no. No amount of love he had for this place would have saved it. Especially not now, not
two years since the grass and the wildflowers and the trees have been stripped away, replaced by a sea of
dead dirt and cracked concrete. He never liked what was done to this place, especially after the colors
on the tarpaulin began to fade and the construction workers with their tools and their vehicles and
their noise began to disappear. He never liked what it did to her. Whatever that meant, at the time. I
had long since lost the time—the energy, really—to remember.

&emsp;Here was another truth: if not for her heart—wrapped in silver sachets and beating out tar—I
would not have realised she was alive.  

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;On the night of my midterm, I drive back to my brother’s wake, and meet with my parents.

&emsp;They hold me close, pat me on the back, rustle my hair, lead me back to his casket, and sit me
down. *Good job. You did well. I’m proud of you. Such a hardworker, we need you now more than ever,*
*okay? You printed what I asked for, right? Yes? Thank you.*

&emsp;My cousin, sitting beside me, turns with a look of concern. No. Horror.

&emsp;I shrug.

&emsp;*You’re so strong.*

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;She is impossible to miss, and she is impossible to look away from.

&emsp;Though her form is made tiny from the distance, and turned blurred from the rain, she makes
herself impossible to look at anything else. She raises her hands, I think, her body twirling round and
round and round, and the field continues to dance with her. Even as the trees shake and their branches
writhe, they echo her choreography. Even as a dark, black substance begins to grow large, larger than I
remembered, she continues to dance.

&emsp;She cocks her head back, and I can *hear* her laugh. It’s pained, tired, the laugh of fracturing
glass. I hear the same every day—in my classmates, in videos, in my own voice. Only ten minutes ago, I
made the same noise. If not for the interruption, I would have made many more.

&emsp;She laughs again, and as her foot kicks at the ground, lightning strikes.

&emsp;She is illuminated, in pain and triumph and laughter, and she is beautiful.

&emsp;*You’re so strong.*

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;On the night we met, she was beautiful.

&emsp;She still is, of course, but in this memory, she is breathtaking. Long flowing hair and skin and
eyes which glitter in the moonlight. She raises her hand to wave, and flowers bloom around her wrist. A
circlet of vines—still flowering, still blooming—wrap around her head, and as she walks towards us, I
catch the smell of petrichor and honey and nectar. She is dressed simply, and if not for her magic (no
other word for it, she had *magic*), I would have convinced myself to have seen her on the street, or at
school, or at Sunday mass.

&emsp;She says something, smiling.

&emsp;Leo translates: “she’s asking who you are.”

&emsp;I shake my head.

&emsp;She frowns.

&emsp;“How about what I am?”

&emsp;Still a no.

&emsp;“I’m sorry.”

&emsp;“It’s okay, I was the same, when we first met,” Leo says, embarrassed. “I still don’t know. She
won’t tell me. She says I have to get better at understanding her first.”
&emsp;“Teach me?” I ask. I was willing to learn. Why wouldn’t I be? This seemed like fun.

&emsp;It was. I didn’t even notice the sun rise, the sky beginning to blush in the wake of dawn.

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;“You get it now, right?”

&emsp;“That was *so* fun.”

&emsp;“*Duh!* So you’ll come back, right?”

&emsp;“Sure.”

&emsp;(“Sure,” then, turns to “maybe later,” to “tomorrow” to “next week” to “when I’m free. I never
had the heart to say no. But how could I go? I had work to do, homework to finish, parents to make
proud. I *wanted* to go. I remember *that*. Just...when I had time. I was tired. I wanted to sleep.)

<p style="text-align: center;">---</p>

&emsp;She’s watching me. As soon as I kneel beside her, her body twitches to awareness—bones
cracking, snapping, contorting—turning to face me. Her mouth moves, and her breath smells like
burning plastic. I can’t understand her. I’m sorry, I don’t remember. I’m sorry.

&emsp;I smile, instead. “You’re alive! That’s—that’s amazing! You’re doing so well.” At her silence, I
continue on: “You’ll be alright, I’m sure. You’re strong aren’t you—and you’ve been here
*forever*—it’s—it’s admirable, really. How do you do it? With all the rain, and the flood,
and—anyway—I’m sure you’ll be alright. You’ll spring back, good as new, right?”

&emsp;A single, heaving breath. Eventually, I feel my awe trickle out, replaced by a thick, familiar
fatigue. I look around, at empty stretches of dirt and concrete, and I can’t recognise the field it used to
be. I look behind me, and see the outline of my village. I need to go home. This visit was long enough
as it was. I had work to do. I stand

&emsp;And feel a pair of hands wrap around my throat. A voice, words I recognise but cannot
comprehend, trickles into my ear. I do not respond. I can’t.

&emsp;I’m pulled under. The ground splits to greet me, and her laughter crackles to welcome me.

&emsp;Then dirt.

&emsp;Then darkness.
