&emsp;The rain had been heaven-sent. There were three projects due the week it came: a video report
on greenhouse gases, a *Laudato Si* poster, a mock business proposal for a sustainable business. All of it
had been pushed back a generous week—and then several weeks more when the school quadrangle
remained underwater. 

&emsp;She couldn't be more grateful. 

&emsp;The last Wednesday of the suspension, she spent swaddled in the heat of her duvet, drifting in
and out of dreams as stone-heavy drops pelted her window. Hot breakfast was waiting for her when she
finally traipsed out of her bedroom—the Spam cooked to a crisp by her yaya, the Delimondo corned
beef still frying in the pan. She was even allowed a sip of her mother's precious civet coffee. By some
divine providence, her father had also been present to greet her for once. He had given a nod, a curt
smile, from behind his Macbook, before turning back to his associate's ChatSlide PowerPoint on
Zoom. 

&emsp;That speck of attention had washed away all residues of irritation from last night, from the
weeks-long radio silence of her project partner Samuel. She'd had high hopes for him, the lone scholar
in their eighth grade classroom. Scrawny and withdrawn and prone to sleeping spells during
classes—not particularly the best at reporting either, if his accent was anything to go by—but excellent
in Science, Math, everywhere else that mattered to her, who had been assigned to work with him. It
was a joke made in poor taste, that was what she thought, when Samuel had cited unstable WiFi
connection, then weak cellular data, and so on and so forth with every passing internal deadline for
their projects. So much for that scholarship. 

&emsp;But whatever. It was water under the bridge. When classes resumed the following Wednesday,
she decided during the ride to school, right there in her father's rumbling Toyota Fortuner, that unlike
what she usually did with indolent groupmates, she would not report nor yell at Samuel. She would
simply ignore him. 

&emsp;And ignore him she did. She turned her head to the side when he took his seat beside her,
angled her chair away at the squelch of his soggy shoes. Samuel, for the most part, looked to be
ignoring her too, slumped as he was against the back of his chair, his thousand-yard stare fixed on the
drip, drip, drip of his own shoes. Why she ever hoped he'd contribute, she didn't know. Samuel hadn't
even the foresight to bring his backpack. 

&emsp;Her other seatmate called her attention, a gaggle of fellow students clustered around their
chair. *What did you do during the break?* 

&emsp;Immediately, her mind drifted back to her impromptu staycation, Samuel's sullen expression
fading to the background of such pleasant memories. As she told them of the civet coffee, of her
marathon naps and her father's smile, of the three projects complete and dry in the safety of her Anello
bag, a sigh nearly escaped her, wistful. *Sana mag-suspend muli.*
