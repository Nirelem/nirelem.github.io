**dal segno (adverb)**
    → used as a direction in music to return to the sign that marks the beginning of a repeat
**fermata (adverb)**
    → a prolongation at the discretion of the performer of a musical note, chord, or rest beyond its
given time value

In one place I am tucked restfully into a corner of a room where mystical craftsmanship of
instruments adorn the walls and an air of soft coziness caresses the audience. Dimly lit lights set the
mood as faces familiar to each performer clump together, plushies tight in their hand, phones upright
and ready to capture a piece of the sedative atmosphere. On a stage embroidered with technologies –
amplifiers, wires, cables and pedals to change up the sound – guitars reverberate at a slow pace;
drumsticks gently pat the snare drum; a silky smooth voice preaches and mournes and gives their
thanks to the ones who showed up. It’s mesmerizing, and the whole room stops and listens with
solemnity. And as I watch the lead singer exert a powerful melancholy to his voice while instruments
blend into something soulful, I think to myself, *Here the music is alright. Here I am at home*. 

And then just outside that foggy glass door is another stage, born from the earth and its wood,
bathing underneath the moonlight. This particular stage has a type of sound that comes up to the
audience, hugs them, and then asks how their day has been. Every pluck and chord (and occasional
percussion slap) comes from the raging acoustics of the guitar, and you just love to see it. A violinist –
it’s so rare to see them – smiles and whisks up a colorful, floral tune. Combined with the pounding
precision of percussion, you come to realize that it’s the traditional folk in all the stylistic and 
astonishingly whimsical elements. Audience members who are probably strangers who walked into the
gig out of curiosity watch on as the awe-inspiring performance sets up one very peculiar atmosphere:
here, lo and behold, we call upon mythologies and worship the might of music. And as I watch the lead
violinist exert a powerful triumph to his violin while a voice soulfully sings, I think to myself, *Here the music is alright. Here I am at home*.

Two sides of the music industry, both working separately yet oddly in tandem with one
another to create something humble and mystifying. The main ragtag group of impassioned artists
holding together a band through duct tape, aspirations, and a wholesome sort of bond went by the
name QNJO. They set up a gig at Motherland at Malingap street for their new EP launch, inviting two
separate artists as opening acts. One of those artists was my very own Japanese- inspired jazz band
modeled after semi-traditional quartets like John Coltrane and Art Bakely. I didn’t really show interest
in staying after our set, so I set out to watch the acoustic performance happening outside. It was
breathtaking and eager, and it was clear all musicians involved held such a high regard for the livelihood
of music. I even befriended their violinist on the grounds that we shared a mutual instrument. We
talked industry careers, orchestral experience, classical background. Never before have I felt closer to
the folk scene; for the first time ever I not only managed to witness them live in action but also got to
connect with one of its performers.

Curiosity crawled its way to my mind during their set, however, so after one last song I slipped
back into the room and placed myself securely in a corner next to my bandmates and boxed in by
strangers. I thought I ascertained that there was already a musical identity I could resonate with. And
then the set started. Thoughts failed me, if they were anything reliable in the first place. Their sound
was mortally sublime and occasionally funky. It was the intersection between distortion’s screeching
riffs and synthesizer’s otherworldly sounds. Most importantly, it was vulnerability; the songs were
built on a strength found in processing the grief of life experience, of which I will not elaborate on and
leave as something to be respected. The set was far beyond the classifications of what was celebration 
and triumph (I would say it’s the opposite spectrum, yet the same effect). It was the unraveling of my
soul that started when the lead singer for QNJO sang a silky song about heartbreak and confession and
wrapped it delicately with neoclassical piano chords, and when I went home from that gig riding in our
drummer’s car, thinking to myself how it was like I had only seconds left to appreciate what came out
of this.

The music industry in the Philippines is wonderful and diverse, and classification of it remains
so faulty, because moments like these that capture the true diversity of music are rare to come. And
while the mental image of impassioned teenagers holding together a band through duct tape,
aspirations, and a wholesome sort of friendship is more common, we cannot neglect the natural
serenity of an acoustic set-up, where one guitarist plays and/or sings their heart out, with perhaps a
percussionist or a lead instrumentalist at their side ready to enhance the music. Nonetheless, they all
share the same goal: to celebrate what is local and what is lovely. And by the end of it, we as an audience
take it all in and feel the triumph, or joy, or sorrow, or melancholy, or the peace and love and everything
that thanks life itself for giving us the senses to hear this piece.

And yet, at the end of the day there exists one indisputable truth: we have to ask ourselves,
“where do we go once the dust settles and the next day steadily approaches?” When the school year was
close to starting, I seldom had time to continue attending gigs like these. With a sea of readings and a
bunch of orgwork stuffing my free time, all I could think about was that moment of peace, of clarity, of
sitting in a performance and listening to its music and waiting patiently as the world passes by and the
night draws on. That is the life that will never detach from me, an appendage that will never be
amputated, even when the reality of my studies looms over me. They were the bursts of quiet energy I
cherished and kept to myself in hopes it would pull me through.

If I live in two times – a past so passionately rooted in music and a present so shakily
unprepared for what’s to come – then I also live in two worlds shifting between that heartfelt message 
of an emerging artist and that delighted smile of an artform long past its prime yet still thriving as
energetically as ever. In a way, they’re their own transition; they embody the crossroads I find myself in
when choosing what kind of musical message I’d like to live by. What I see today as triumph, I see
tomorrow as sorrow; what appeared to be downtrodden yesterday, will appear to be happier next time.
Still, they are always my home, and they’ll never truly fade away when I move forward in life, much less
when I shift from one area of music to another. There’s a kind of freedom in getting to choose how
you want your retrospective to be shaped like. It brings closure to the nagging fear that I’ve prioritized
one musical lifestyle over another, and it ends up unifying both sides of sublimity – the groovy and the
teary eyed – into one concrete reassurance: everything will turn out fine, I believe. So until I get to step
into another room, another stage, another place to appreciate the arts and love them so fully, I walk
shakily yet somehow steadily towards life with the embers of that sound which I hold there.

I never really got to see the end of the folk gig, since I had to leave early with my drummer to
be dropped off at my townhouse. However, as I left, I waved a little goodbye to the violinist who,
smiling, waved bye back. For all the complexities of performance and musicianship that have been
described, it was refreshing to just have that one moment of casualty to truly show how, at the end of
the day, music was something communal that we could always go back to.

**<u>AUTHOR’S STATEMENT:</u>**
This piece was made with one idea in mind: combining two facets of music and turning them into a
lesson on longevity. I wanted to establish that music, regardless of its form, is something that never
quite leaves you, quietly supporting you during the stressful times of life through the impact attending
a gig has on you. I recognize that the piece may be mistaken for following a dichotomy type of theme,
but I will say that most of it is intended to come off as a reflection of the “downtime” passionate artists
have by watching each other’s gigs and supporting each other in the industry, turning such gigs into a
sweet memory before it’s back to business. In a way, music becomes that intersection between past and
present (and most possibly the future) that touches both your soul and your mind.
